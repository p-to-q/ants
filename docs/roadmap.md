## What the search is for, stated precisely (2026-10-17)

"Find the best model quickly", read as a shortest-path problem, is misleading. Dijkstra and A* run on a known graph with known edge costs; here the landscape is unknown, every measurement costs compute, and measurements are noisy. The nearer description is: **search an unknown, expensive, noisy landscape while measuring it.** No Free Lunch (Wolpert and Macready, 1997) says that, averaged over all problems, no search beats another; the later qualification (Igel and Toussaint) is that it does not hold on structured classes. So being faster than random search is a statement about the structure of this problem, and has to be earned from that structure and shown by experiment ([search-landscape](research/search-landscape.md)).

The target, restated: **within a fixed compute budget, reach a model close to the best, with high probability, as early as possible, and with evidence that survives verification.** Four quantities, none optional:

| quantity | measured as | established tool | state |
|---|---|---|---|
| speed | core-hours until the best-so-far first reaches a stated target | runtime-to-target; anytime curves | recorded per trial (time and cost are in the ledger); not reported |
| quality | relative error at the target, per cap, family-weighted | the metric already in use | reported (best per cap) |
| probability | the fraction of independent seeds that reach the target within the budget, with an interval; the empirical distribution of runtimes over seeds and targets | ECDF of runtimes and data profiles (the COCO / BBOB and Moré–Wild practice) | **not measurable**: one seed per arm; needs step 2 |
| evidence | reaching the target on data the search never saw (confirm, sealed look through the gate), and a result another party can re-verify | gate, bundle, audit | gate exists and is unwired (27); bundle and audit planned (24, 29) |

Two cautions that follow. First, the optimum is unknown, so "near-optimal" can only mean "near the best any method has found so far"; targets are therefore set relative to the pooled best of all runs and stated before the comparison (a ladder, for example 5%, 2%, 1%), and the word optimal is not used. Second, a method that is fast to a target with probability 0.3 and one that is slower with probability 0.9 are different results; a single "best value after N hours" hides this, which is why one seed per arm cannot support a claim about search.

The structure that can be used, and how much of it is used today:

| structure | used today | not yet |
|---|---|---|
| discrete structure plus continuous parameters, some levers valid only under some cores (conditional space) | operators respect cores in mutation and crossover | a surrogate that handles conditionality (TPE is in; SMAC-style random forest is not): steps 17, 18 |
| inheritance between models (lineages) | delayed lineage credit; stepping stones | credit from measured landscape, not a fixed discount: step 19 |
| s0, s1, s2 as measurements of one target at rising cost | quantile promotion per cap | s1 and s2 agree at Spearman 0.99 while s2 costs 45% of the CPU, so the ladder as built is nearly one fidelity; real fidelity here is datasets, horizons and evaluation spans, not epochs (nothing is trained for epochs): step 18 |
| error against parameter count | four caps as separate bins | one two-objective frontier with a hypervolume signal: step 18 |
| closed-form fitting makes a trial cheap | the whole design | cost-aware choice (the `ens` lever triples cost and nothing prices it): step 18 |

## The rule for anything that adapts: how the next round searches may change, what counts as success may not (owner, 2026-10-17)

| may change from one round to the next (inside stated bounds, every change a logged event with the policy version, step 35) | fixed for an epoch (changing any of it is a human decision that starts a new epoch) |
|---|---|
| annealing temperature and its reheat rule | the metric, its aggregation and the family weights (`rel_mse`, with `rel_mae` as a guard) |
| mutation scale, crossover and restart rates, population size inside the stated range | the datasets and their roles (development, confirm, sealed), the stages and what each one measures |
| the share each search operator gets (the bandit already moves it; a policy may move its priors and limits) | the parameter caps and how a parameter is counted |
| which region of the stated space is studied now (levers and ranges weighted or narrowed, never extended beyond the contract's space) | the promotion rule and its thresholds, and the definition of a best-so-far (they sit in the contract) |
| whether a language model is called at all in a round, and how often (inside the floor an equal-budget comparison fixes) | the judge, the gate and the sealed-look accounting, the targets of step 31, the budget definitions, the seed lists |
| the order and batching of the next experiments | anything that decides whether a result counts: a policy can never mark its own trial as better, skip a stage, re-run for a luckier seed or choose which result is reported |

Reasons, so that the line is not drawn by habit. A system that can move the finish line will move it, without intent, whenever moving it is the cheaper way to look better; the cure is that the finish line is outside everything that learns. And a change of the second column is a change of experiment: results before and after cannot be pooled, which is what the epoch records. Two consequences for the plan. In a comparison between arms, the first column is frozen or is itself the thing compared (a policy that adapts is an arm, with its bounds written in the arm definition so `ants doctor` can see a change); an adaptive arm may not be set against a fixed one and read as if only the adaptation differed unless the budget is equal on both axes of step 31. And the authority to move the first column belongs to the scheduler and its controllers (deterministic code with bounds), not to the proposing model: the model may add its observation to the information layer of step 30, and the controller may use it, but the model does not turn the dials.

## Evaluation speed: where the time goes (2026-10-17)

Every budget in this project is CPU-seconds, so the cost of one evaluation is the most direct lever there is. A profile of one second-stage evaluation (`evaluate_stage_catalog(spec, "s1")`, a specification from the top plateau: period core, lookback 640, ensemble of 3, 4 datasets × 4 horizons, one thread on the development computer) says 4.6 s in total:

| where | seconds | share | what it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| `data.sample_windows` (144 calls, 16 ms each) | 2.36 | **51%** | builds an int64 index matrix of (windows × (lookback + horizon)) and gathers the windows with fancy indexing; the closed-form solve is not the cost |
| `Core.predict` and `_raw_pred` | 0.68 | 15% | forecasting the validation windows member by member |
| `_prep`, `reshape`, `numpy.array` | 0.8 | 17% | conversions and copies around the fits and the scoring |
| `_fit_once` (108 fits) | 0.91 | 20% | the ridge solves themselves (these include their own calls to the pieces above) |

A window builder that takes a no-copy `sliding_window_view` of the series and gathers only the windows themselves returns **bit-identical** arrays (same values, same dtype, same order; checked on 12 combinations of series length, channel count, lookback and horizon, with and without a cap) and takes 3 to 6 times less time (16 ms to 3 ms at the sizes the stages use; `MAX_WINDOW_ELEMS` = 4 M elements bounds the windows). Applied to the profile above it lowers an evaluation from about 4.6 s to about 2.7 s, which is 1.7 times more trials for the same CPU, before the other items are looked at.

`data.py` is in the contract's `code_paths`, so the fingerprint, and with it the epoch id, would change although every measurement stays the same. Rule proposed (step 48): an evaluator change that is *performance only* may carry an epoch if a **golden replay** shows bit-identical metrics: the contract lists the fingerprints it is proven equivalent to (`evaluator_equivalent = [...]`), the epoch id is computed from the oldest of them, and the proof (the specifications replayed, their metrics before and after, the two fingerprints) is a file in the repository and a row in the epochs table. Without such a proof a change of any `code_paths` file is a new epoch, as today. Until the rule exists the faster builder waits for the epoch-3 package (45).

## Fit, tune, finish: three tools, each measured alone (2026-10-17)

SearchCast (Huang, Xu and Darlow, arXiv 2606.27282; text read in full on 2026-10-17) is the closest published pipeline to the part of ants that fits and tunes. What it does, from the paper: closed-form Ridge forecasters; a search, per cell of a horizon × series grouping, with Optuna's TPE sampler over the lookback (32 to 2048, log scale), the normalisation scope and method including a **trailing fraction r of the context** (0.001 to 1, log scale; r = 1 is ordinary instance normalisation), augmentation in the **time or the frequency domain** (noise scale 0.001 to 0.5, or none), with **the regularisation strength chosen inside every trial from a grid of 21 values (10^-6 to 10^3) on the validation loss, so that it costs no search**; three-fold expanding-window validation; reductions of the average MSE over ordinary least squares of 4.3% (ETTh1), 4.8% (ETTm2) and 14.8% (Exchange) in the text, wins over the nonlinear baselines on average on six of eight datasets, losses to PatchTST on Electricity (tie) and Traffic. It is a preprint, reports no parameter counts, runs on a GPU at milliseconds a trial, and its code is public.

The question put to ants: should gradient training, ridge regression and tuning each be a tool of its own? The answer the evidence supports, tool by tool:

| tool | what it is here | decision | evidence | step |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fit** (ridge) | the closed-form solve inside the gym, with the strength as an outer lever `ridge` searched by the black-box optimiser | **yes, as an inner path, expecting little**: one decomposition per fold gives the whole path of strengths, the best is chosen on the validation windows, and `ridge` leaves the search space. The forest on our own ledger already ranks `ridge` among the most used features, which is search effort spent on something a solve can do. Gaussian input noise (`aug_sigma`) is, in expectation, a ridge penalty (Bishop 1995), so it overlaps with the path and is reviewed with it | SearchCast §3.2; ledgers; **measured 2026-10-17** (`scripts/exp_ridge_path.py`): 24 specifications from the top plateau, the rest of the top quarter and the second quarter of two ledgers, each rerun at s1 with `ridge` replaced by a grid of 13 values (1e-3 to 1e3) and by 1e-6: the best grid value lowers s1 `rel_mse` by 0.15% (top plateau, 8 specs), 0.15% (next, 8) and 0.72% (second quarter, 8) against the strength the search had chosen, and 0.78%, 0.56%, 0.22% against 1e-6, which is ordinary least squares in practice; for half the specifications the best value lies more than half a decade from the searched one. The surface is flat: in this regime (few parameters, structured models) the strength hardly matters, unlike SearchCast's 4 to 15% over OLS, which comes from searching the preprocessing as well. Caveats: 24 specifications, most of them near one lineage, s1 only | 46 |
| **Tune** (the search) | ants' search layer; SearchCast's tuner is Optuna's TPE per cell | **already a tool**; the two things SearchCast has that ants lacks are the **trailing-fraction ratio r** of the normalisation (ants has `none`, `last`, `mean`, `revin`) and **frequency-domain augmentation**, and its **grouping** of horizons and series with separate hyperparameters per cell (ants uses one specification for all datasets and horizons; the parameter cap makes per-cell models a trade-off against size) | SearchCast Tables, Figures 1 to 7 | 46 (levers), later (grouping) |
| **Finish** (gradient training) | none today; gradient-trained steps exist only as pieces (`distill_lambda`, `pretrain`, `prune` and `quant` simulated in closed form) | **not a search dimension; a finisher for finalists**. For the linear class the closed form is at least as good as stochastic gradient descent in 72% of the settings tested (Toner and Darlow, ICML 2024) and SearchCast does not train by gradient at all; gradient steps earn their place where the closed form cannot express the model: quantisation-aware training, pruning followed by retraining, learned frequencies. Tested as a hand-off on the finalists of each cap against a closed-form refit at the same size | Toner and Darlow; [training-lifecycle](research/training-lifecycle.md) | 47 |

The rule that follows from the table: every tool gets an ablation of its own on the same specifications and the same caps, in a ladder, so that the gain is attributed to something. The ladder: ordinary least squares (ridge 10^-6); the searched `ridge` as today; the inner path; the inner path plus the trailing-fraction ratio; plus frequency augmentation; plus grouping; plus the finisher. Each rung is a number at each cap, with seeds.

**The search core these point to** (a hypothesis to test, not a result): one trusted fidelity at which ranking is done (today s1) with s2 replaced by an independent check on finalists; a **surrogate** (random forest with an uncertainty estimate, as in SMAC, fitted on the s1 results and on the cost) that screens many cheap proposals and sends the best few per CPU-second to evaluation (the pattern of BANANAS: mutate many, rank by a predictor with Thompson sampling, evaluate few, arXiv 1910.11858); **local search by aging evolution as the proposal engine, started from K independent roots** (local search is a strong baseline on NAS benchmarks, White, Nolen and Savani, arXiv 2005.02960, though it did worse than random on the largest space they tried); per-cap archives with downward seeding; and the nine operators kept as optional generators and as the control arm, each earning its place in the leave-one-out ablation of step 17. [optimiser-implementations](research/optimiser-implementations.md) reads the source of Optuna, SMAC3, DEHB, BOHB, HEBO, Nevergrad, pyribs, OpenEvolve, ShinkaEvolve and BANANAS and ranks six changes (candidate pool ranked by the surrogate, Thompson ranking per CPU-second, several aging-evolution roots, conditional-aware TPE, parent weights, batch diversification). Optuna's `AutoSampler` (OptunaHub) makes the same kind of choice by rule (TPE for conditional spaces, GP for continuous, NSGA for several objectives, by budget). Neural surrogates (PFNs4BO, arXiv 2305.17535) and model-written proposals are not first choices here: the single study found that a model's warm start was a default configuration and that classical search caught the model within five evaluations (arXiv 2606.21641, eight tabular datasets). The old way stays available as the `portfolio` arm.

A researcher that writes code, not specs, needs the operating-system isolation of step 25 first. ## Shared without a model: what the arms can exchange that code computes and checks (2026-10-17)

The information layer of step 30 does not need a language model to be useful. Each object below is computed from the ledgers by deterministic code, carries the trial ids it rests on, and can be recomputed by anyone; none of it is an instruction. In rough order of value for the cost:

| shared object | computed from | what another searcher can do with it | how it is checked |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Evaluation cache.** The result of (epoch, stage, spec key, seed) is the same for every arm because the gym is deterministic | finished trials of every arm, same epoch and same data manifest | skip a specification another arm already measured: zero compute for a repeat; the 35 to 45% coarse duplicates are partly this | `ants reverify --sample N` (step 24) re-evaluates a sample and compares bit for bit; a mismatch switches the cache off |
| **Mutation-effect atlas.** Every child of a lineage differs from its parent by recorded edits (`origin_key`, `edit`), so each child-parent pair is a controlled one-lever experiment | the same columns the agent already reads | a table: changing lever L from a to b, in this core and this cap, moved the metric by this much (paired differences, interval); it replaces guessing which lever matters | prospective only: an entry is a hypothesis until a batch made after it was written confirms it; multiple testing controlled over the declared family |
| **Lever importance and interactions; neutral levers** (those the spec key already collapses) | step 21's analysis | skip levers that change nothing here; look first where two levers interact | recomputed per epoch; the analysis is a function of the ledger |
| **Exhausted and empty regions.** Cells (structure niche × cap bin) with many trials and no gain; cells with none | the quality-diversity grid `qd` already keeps | send effort to empty cells; stop revisiting exhausted ones | counts and best value per cell, inspectable |
| **A shared surrogate and a shared cost model.** One forest or density fitted on the pooled trials; one predictor of `cpu_s` and memory from the lever values (the `ens` lever triples cost) | pooled finished trials | every searcher can rank proposals by predicted value per predicted second | held-out rank correlation reported with the model (step 22) |
| **Stage calibration.** Where the first stage can be trusted: rank correlation s0 to s1 to s2 per cap and per core, and the share of the eventual top quartile that s0 rejects | the same ledgers | decide where the cheap stage may filter and where it must not | the unselected-sample measurement of step 5 |
| **Operator credit as a prior.** Posterior of each operator's discovery rate per cap and stage | the bandit state of the other arms | warm-start a new arm's bandit, discounted | the decision log (step 35) shows what the prior did |
| **Failure map.** Regions that error, run out of memory or break the cap | `error` and `n_params` of finished trials | never propose them again; the cap can be checked before an evaluation, which is deterministic | counts and the error text |
| **Transfer table.** Which lever settings help on which data family and which dataset | per-dataset metrics of the deepest stage | a finding is portable or local, and says which; guards against steering four datasets only | needs confirm data for the portable claims, so only a report until the gate is wired |
| **Refutation register.** Hypotheses that a prospective test did not support, with the test | the atlas and its follow-up batches | nobody spends a batch on a settled question; refutations are kept as carefully as successes | the test, its data and its result are rows, not prose |
| **Region claims with a lease.** An arm marks a cell it is exploring for a stated time (the fencing-token pattern the ledger already uses) | a small claims table | two arms do not explore the same cell at once | expiry; claims never block, they only reorder |

Rules for all of them. (1) **Generated and tested on different trials**: a pattern mined from a window of the ledger is a hypothesis, and it counts only if trials created after it was written support it; otherwise the miner is a source of false findings by the look-elsewhere effect. (2) **Multiple testing**: tests are declared as families beforehand and a false-discovery-rate rule (Benjamini–Hochberg) is applied within a family. (3) **Selection bias**: lineage edits are chosen by operators that favour promising levers, so the atlas is observational; a designed batch (one lever flipped on matched parents) is what turns an entry into evidence. (4) **Sharing makes arms dependent**: any comparison of arms keeps an unshared control, and the evaluation cache, which changes how a budget is spent, is part of the arm definition: a hit costs no compute and is counted apart, so the equal-budget tables of step 31 can be read with and without it. (5) Nothing here writes into another arm's state; readers get views.

## What the reward should be (2026-10-17)

Today the bandit's reward is **promotion**: a proposal counts as a success when its cheap-stage value ranks in the top quarter of its cap bin and it moves up a stage. That rewards looking good at the cheap stage, which keeps about half of the true top quartile and agrees with the next stage mostly because both read the same data family. It is a proxy for progress, and an operator can win credit by producing specs the cheap stage likes.

The target, in the order of how much it can be trusted:

| reward | what it measures | where it fails | step |
|---|---|---|---|
| promoted (today) | cheap-stage rank | the proxy is weak and the stages are redundant | shipped |
| improved the best of its cap at the deepest stage | real progress at one cap | ignores the other caps and the size axis; one winner takes all the credit | 18 |
| **gain in dominated hypervolume of the (error, parameters) front, measured at the deepest stage, per CPU-second** | progress on the whole frontier, charged for what it cost | needs a reference point and the deepest-stage run to finish (delayed credit) | 18, 31, 35 |
| the same, counted only after the model is confirmed on held-out data | progress that survives a second look | confirm looks are counted and limited | 27, 37 |

Credit is delayed and goes to the lineage (route) that produced the model, with the decision log of step 35 as its evidence. The cheap-stage proxy stays as a *shaping* term with a small weight, and is checked against the deep reward on the finished ledgers (step 22) before it is trusted.

## Search groups, arms and operators: what the words mean here (2026-10-17)

* An **operator** (random, sweep, evolution mutate or cross, differential evolution, CEM, ant colony, annealing, quality-diversity, TPE, the model operator, the agent operator) turns the history into a batch of candidate specs. It has no memory beyond what its algorithm keeps, no budget, and no opinion about depth.
* A **portfolio** is a set of operators plus a scheduler (a Thompson bandit with floors) that decides how many of the next proposals each operator gets, and a cascade rule that decides which proposals go deeper.
* An **arm** (a "search group" in the interface) is one complete, separately running system: its own ledger, history and portfolio. The three arms on the server are `portfolio` (no model operators), `portfolio_llm` (adds a one-shot model operator) and `portfolio_agent` (adds the agent operator, which reads the ledger with tools). They do not cooperate: no arm sees another's trials. They exist so that one question can be asked at equal budget: *does adding a model-driven operator change what the same portfolio finds?* The layering an outside reviewer describes (researchers inside one system, a scheduler above them) is a different design, steps 30 to 33, and is not built.
* What the model operators do today: the one-shot operator is given a text summary of the best specs and failures and returns candidate specs; the agent operator may call up to six read-only tools over its own ledger and then returns candidates. Neither asks for a direction, a stage or a budget, and neither judges anything: the cascade judges.
* **Jev** is a different thing again. `src/antslab/judge.py` holds a client for Jev, an external discriminative decision service (a "System-One" model that answers choice questions with probabilities), and an authority ladder: every prediction is logged as `shadow`, scored later against the deterministic outcome, and only a calibrated judge (AUC, Brier, ECE) may become `advisory` and then `delegated`; the final claim is never delegated. Nothing in the running loop calls it, and no Jev endpoint is configured on the server, so it was not used. It is the natural place for the scheduler's "which direction is worth deep validation" question once step 35 gives it logged decisions to learn from.

## The training lifecycle, and where ants stands (2026-10-17)

The surveys R1 to R13 read about *search*: hyperparameter and architecture search, evolution, quality-diversity, multi-fidelity methods, agents that propose. They did not read about *training*. The honest map:

| stage | what it is in general | in ants today | in the plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| pre-training | learn general representations on very large, varied data | none; every model starts from nothing and is fitted on one dataset | a published foundation model may serve as a *reference* (step 14), not as a part |
| continued pre-training and adaptation | adapt a general model to a domain | none | out of scope until a general model is a part of the system |
| supervised fine-tuning | fit the target on labelled data | the closed-form ridge fit *is* supervised fitting of a small model; no gradient steps | step 23, separate gym |
| parameter-efficient adaptation (adapters, low-rank) | train few parameters on top of a large frozen model | none | step 23, a gradient gym with a small adapter inside a cap |
| reinforcement or preference learning | improve a policy from a reward | none; the loop's own feedback is a bandit and an evolutionary selection, which is not RL on a model | only if a task has a reward the model acts on; not for forecasting error |
| distillation, pruning, quantisation, quantisation-aware training | make a trained model smaller or cheaper | levers in the search (prune ratio, quantisation, distillation weight), simulated in NumPy; no model exported | step 26 (export and measure), step 23 (compress from large) |
| full-parameter training at small scale | gradient training of all weights of a small model | none; the closed form replaces it for the linear and Fourier cores | step 23 |
| train-small against shrink-large | which route gives the better model at a fixed size | not compared | step 23 is exactly this comparison |

Where ants *does* learn: the search policy (operator shares, lineage credit, pheromone), which is learning about where good models are, not training a model. R14 is read before any stage above is added; the first question for each is whether it changes what the search is for (a model at a size) or only how a model is produced.

Change: refuse to start an arm whose definition hash differs from the ledger's last `daemon_started`, unless `--new-definition` is given |

Training results are kept in three places: the ledger (every trial, spec and metric, chained), the per-trial files, and the verified backups; the first epoch's runs are in `/var/lib/ants/archive/old-runs` untouched. What a harness change can do to them is in [upgrading](upgrading.md). Whether the new epoch is better than the old one is not known yet: the changes were made because the first epoch had not searched two of four caps and never ran the model operator, so the two epochs answer different questions; comparing their best values would compare protocols, not searches.

## How the comparison is kept honest

1. N, the seed list and the test are written to a file before the runs start; seeds are interleaved across arms; nobody looks at the comparison until all runs finish.
2. Primary metric: best rel_mse at 150 deepest-stage evaluations in the ≤1000 and ≤3000 bins; paired Wilcoxon per seed, agent against llm and agent against plain, Holm correction; medians and a bootstrap interval over seeds.
3. Between-run spread from three data points is about 0.0024 in best rel_mse, so differences of 0.003 need about 10 seeds and 0.005 about 4; the plan uses 8.
4. Report equal evaluations and equal CPU; stop a run only for infrastructure faults (model yield under 5% of proposals, an endpoint down), never for efficacy.
5. A change to promotion, stages or data is a new epoch; results of different epochs are never pooled (`ants best`, `top` and the interface read the current epoch only).
